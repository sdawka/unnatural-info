# UNNATURAL: research and 50 improvements

Research date: 21 September 2026.

This report synthesizes separate reviews by **Luna** (reader entry, comparable projects, interactive learning), **Terra** (prior work, chapter coverage, everyday use), and **Sol** (arguments, scientific claims, internal consistency), followed by a combined editorial review.

The review covers the local homepage, Explore, About, Reading, all **24 numbered chapters**, the preface, prologue, epilogue, relevant wiki pages, and the analysis assistant's framework instructions. The public domain could not be retrieved through the web tool, so observations about your site are based on the checked source. Animation timing is inferred from code; no reader study or browser usability test was conducted. External research used project pages, original papers, scholarly reference articles, and publisher descriptions. This is an editorial audit of a developing outline, not a claim to have read every cited book in full.

## The central recommendation

Make the reader's journey visible before explaining the system:

**Notice what moves you → examine how you make sense of things → choose how to respond.**

Your most useful promise is already in the prologue: a chapter should give the reader something to look at, look for, or do. On the homepage, that promise is hidden behind a reveal and the phrases “free admin access to the information realm” and “pattern in substrate.” Those phrases require familiarity with the book before they make sense.

The manuscript has a second mismatch: its closing qualifications are often more careful than its opening claims. Preserve the curiosity and the broad connections, and bring that care into the main sentences. Readers should be able to use the distinctions while disagreeing with your account of their ultimate origin.

The bibliography is already extensive. Most of the intellectual improvement will come from making selected sources do visible work: an example, an objection, an alternative explanation, or a practice. Another long reading list would do little for the intended audience.

## A concrete opening to consider

> **Why do I keep living by ideas I never chose?**
>
> UNNATURAL is an unfinished online book about what moves us, how we make sense of the world, and how we can live more deliberately.
>
> Through ordinary situations—an argument, a habit, a job, a meal—you'll practise noticing what you want, questioning the stories you live by, and trying small changes.
>
> No philosophy background needed. Start with one everyday problem, then follow the ideas as far as you like.
>
> **Start reading** · **See the chapters**

This is proposed copy, not a claim that the current outline already delivers all these exercises. Introduce it alongside a finished opening sample. A possible first scene: a friend has not replied, you decide they do not care, and your next message makes things worse. Wanting, interpretation, uncertainty, and action are already present before any new vocabulary appears.

## The 50 improvements

The categories contain 10 General, 10 Content, 14 Argument, 6 Practicality, and 10 Interactive Ideas. **Start** identifies the first changes worth making. Chapter references refer to the current numbering.

### General — 1–10

1. **Start — Put a human benefit in the first screen.** Tell readers this is an online book for understanding their wants, assumptions, choices, and shared world. Follow the existential hook with a concrete result. “Free admin access” currently introduces both jargon and an exaggerated suggestion of control. Location: homepage and prologue.

2. **Start — Give the reader a small experience before the cosmology.** Open with the unanswered-message scene, or a familiar disagreement. Have the reader identify the event and the story they added. Move the Big Bang preface behind an optional origin-story link if it interrupts this entry. The first page should demonstrate the kind of seeing the rest of the book develops.

3. **Start — Make reading immediately available.** The source schedules 2.5 seconds before the reveal and another 4.2 seconds before showing ENTER, with a possible view-transition delay. Keep a visible Start reading link from the beginning. The source also hides both slides without JavaScript and binds every key to skip/navigation; preserve normal keyboard use and make the content available independently of the animation.

4. **Put ordinary names before house vocabulary.** Present the acts as “What moves us,” “How we make sense of things,” and “How we learn, create, and change.” Keep Volo Ergo Sum, init.d, blades, and MMM as secondary names. Replace “substrate” with “the material carrying a pattern” on first use. A reader should not need Unix knowledge to understand the second act.

5. **Start — Reconcile the book's versions.** Explore says 22 chapters; there are 24 numbered chapters plus framing material. About, Explore, later chapters, wiki pages, and the analysis assistant still use the older Measure–Model–Manifest framing, while chapters 13–17 introduce three different cycles. Chapter 24 says “eighteen chapters.” Establish one vocabulary and sequence, then update all summaries and cross-references.

6. **Make the unfinished state useful information.** Distinguish outline, readable draft, and revised chapter. Estimate reading time from the prose actually available and describe exercise time separately. Give readers one finished sample so they can judge the intended experience. Avoid a large reading-time promise for a path that currently consists mostly of outlines.

7. **Make the prologue's method visible at the claim.** Use brief labels such as “something you can notice,” “a proposed explanation,” and “an open question.” Replace the defensive “unscientific empirical” explanation with a clear account of lived observation, research, interpretation, and revision. A reader's introspection can test an experience claim; it cannot establish a universal biological history.

8. **Give each chapter one dependable shape.** Everyday scene → question → claim → explanation → strongest objection → small practice → next question. Use one or two sources beside the relevant claim, with the longer bibliography optional. [OpenStax's structure](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/preface) is a useful reference; the intended tone can remain personal and informal.

9. **Make the route and the reason for its order explicit.** Show what each act changes in the reader's understanding. Offer a main sequential path and a few problem entrances, such as disagreement, identity, and learning. Include a way to resume. Explain why a chapter about distinctions comes after one about wanting, rather than relying on readers to infer the dependency.

10. **Test comprehension with a handful of new readers.** Show the entry briefly and ask them to explain what this is, whom it is for, what it promises, and where they would start. Then ask one reader to explain a chapter in their own words. Record their language and confusion. These would be exploratory checks, not evidence of broad effectiveness or a statistically validated conversion test.

### Content: prior work to add or actually use — 11–20

“Already listed” means the source exists somewhere in the chapters, wiki, or Reading page; its useful contribution is not yet adequately developed in the prose. “New” means it was not found in that checked material.

11. **Use wanting, liking, and endorsing as different questions.** **Already listed:** Berridge and Frankfurt. In chapters 1, 3, and 7, use the reader who keeps scrolling after it stops being enjoyable, while wishing they wanted to stop. Berridge's research separates incentive wanting from pleasure; Frankfurt provides a philosophical route into which desires we identify with. The scrolling example is an illustration, not a diagnosis. [Berridge and Robinson](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5171207/).

12. **Give meaning and love a serious alternative to expansion.** **Already listed:** Susan Wolf, Murdoch, and de Beauvoir. In chapters 3, 16, and 23, include caring for someone whose condition will not improve, maintaining a friendship, or enjoying music without becoming better at anything. Ask whether meaningful engagement depends on worthwhile objects and relationships as well as sustained desire. [Wolf's lectures](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Wolf%20-%202007%20-%20Meaning%20in%20Life%20and%20Why%20It%20Matters.pdf).

13. **Add whose testimony gets counted.** **New:** Miranda Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice*. Chapters 4, 11, and 24 need more than many reports converging: some people are ignored because of who they are, and some lack shared language for their experience. Use a junior worker whose accurate warning gains credibility only when a manager repeats it. [Fricker's book](https://academic.oup.com/book/32817).

14. **Bring in an alternative to the mind-as-map account.** **Already listed:** Gibson and embodied/enactive approaches. In chapters 5–6, use catching a ball or finding a door handle: action and environmental information can guide someone without an explicit internal diagram. Explain that your “map” is a helpful lens within a live philosophical debate. Acknowledge where ecological and representational accounts disagree. [Gibson's book](https://www.routledge.com/The-Ecological-Approach-to-Visual-Perception-Classic-Edition/Gibson/p/book/9781848725782).

15. **Show how classifications allocate consequences.** **Already listed:** Bowker and Star, Rosch, Hacking, and Scott. Chapters 8–11 should follow a form whose options do not fit a person, or a workplace score that rewards speed while hiding care. Describe who made the categories, what they enable, and who pays for the mismatch. This gives thingification ethical and practical substance. [Bowker and Star](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262522953/sorting-things-out/).

16. **Add a positive account of people governing something together.** **New:** Elinor Ostrom. Chapters 9, 19, and 20 need a case between the lone individual and all civilization. Use a shared garden: membership, maintenance, monitoring, disagreement, and changing a rule. Ostrom gives participation and correction concrete institutional detail. Do not turn her context-sensitive findings into a universal recipe. [Published Nobel lecture](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.100.3.641).

17. **Explain what your cycles contribute beyond earlier feedback models.** **Already listed:** Ashby, Kolb, Argyris and Schön. In chapters 13–18, compare correcting an action with reconsidering the goal that made it seem necessary. A reader might learn to answer messages faster, then ask why being always available became the goal. Let your distinctive contribution be the accessible synthesis and lived examples; similarity to older models does not validate the whole framework. [Argyris's paper](https://www.iveypublishing.ca/s/product/double-loop-learning-in-organizations/01t5c00000CwWbwAAF).

18. **Add memory and retrieval to the learning chapter.** **New:** Roediger and Karpicke's retrieval-practice research. Chapter 14 discusses knowledge while barely teaching readers how to retain it. Ask them to close the explanation, retrieve its point, check it, and revisit it later. Evidence for retention supports this design choice; it does not establish that recall changes a worldview. [Original experiment](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x/).

19. **Add a concrete bridge from intention to action.** **New:** Gollwitzer and Sheeran on implementation intentions. Chapters 15 and 23 could move from “I should listen better” to “If I notice myself preparing a rebuttal, I will first summarize what I heard.” Include a cue, a manageable action, and a later check. Such planning has empirical support without guaranteeing success in any particular situation. [Authors' publication page](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes).

20. **Use spiritual practices in their own terms before mapping them to MMM.** **New:** Hadot as an organizing source; **already listed:** Epictetus and Buddhist/contemplative traditions. In chapters 16 and 22, work through two named practices and their different aims. For example, compare attention to judgment in [Epictetus](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html) with observation of body, feeling, and mind in [MN 10](https://suttacentral.net/mn10/en/sujato?lang=en). Mark the MMM comparison as your interpretation. [Hadot](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/philosophy-as-a-way-of-life-978-0-631-18033-3) helps connect philosophy with repeated practice.

### Argument — 21–34

These distinguish errors, unsupported claims, and contestable interpretations. A philosophical position can be worth developing without being scientifically demonstrated. Where the manuscript already gives an appropriate qualification, the repair is to put it into the positive claim as well.

21. **Start — Define will without claiming too much from it.** Chapters 1–2 move between physiological regulation, felt desire, agency, and the origin of difference. Define minimal “will” as your functional term for orientation; distinguish it from conscious choice. Narrow the Damasio claim to particular decision impairments. Resolve the apparent sequence conflict: toward/away is foundational in chapter 1 but introduced after self and model in chapter 7. These can be explanatory lenses without a proven biological order. [Damasio's hypothesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8941953/).

22. **Start — Separate a shared account of the world from the world's existence.** Chapter 4 says the shared world disappears when minds stop communicating; chapter 10 recognizes an independent physical world. Say that a shared interpretation or coordinated practice may dissolve. The mountain remains. Similarly, chapter 9 should distinguish the decisions involved in naming an object from its physically robust structure. Keep the existing rejection of consensus-as-truth and make the main prose match it.

23. **Remove the unnecessary infinite-universe premise.** Chapters 5 and 11 assert universal fractality, chaos, and effectively infinite information in a finite region. Those conclusions do not follow from being able to zoom across familiar scales. “The available detail exceeds what this observer can use” does all the work you need. Keep fractals and chaotic systems as bounded examples, with their conditions specified. [Scholarly account of chaos](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chaos/).

24. **Distinguish “not X” from “the opposite of X.”** Chapter 8 moves from a logical partition to a universal claim about opposing pairs and a prior experiential unity. Not-hot includes warm and cold; not-friend includes stranger and enemy. Explain which distinctions have gradations, several categories, or genuinely opposed poles. Pirsig's prior unity can remain an attributed philosophical proposal; the logical partition does not prove it.

25. **Keep representation, truth, and existence apart.** Chapters 6, 9, 10, and 12 use information to cover models, beliefs, institutions, experience, and knowledge. A false map exists; the place it depicts may not. A contract depends on organized practices; a rock does not depend on the same kind of agreement. Distinguish these with one worked example. Substrate dependence and multiple levels of explanation do not establish a second substance. [Semantic information and its disputed definitions](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/information-semantic/).

26. **Correct Gödel and limit what the mathematical history establishes.** Chapter 12 blends the first and second incompleteness theorems and omits effective axiomatization. State their separate claims with the relevant arithmetic and consistency conditions. Remove the inference that they establish a limit on every self-model. The chapter's own warnings against misuse are sound. Likewise, Mesopotamian counting practices illustrate one historical lineage; they do not establish the origin of all mathematics or solve Wigner's puzzle. [Gödel reference](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/goedel-incompleteness/).

27. **Distinguish learning, prediction, intervention, and control.** Chapter 14 makes control the endpoint of learning and implies changing A then observing B establishes causation. Astronomy provides knowledge without manipulating stars. A household experiment can be misleading if several things change together. Say that interventions can help identify causes when their design and assumptions support the inference; observational causal inference also needs assumptions. [Interventionist causation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/causation-mani/).

28. **Start — Remove the unsupported maturity ladder.** Chapters 13–17 say the cycles are not a hierarchy, then describe fixed developmental progress and regression under stress. The proposed stages are not established by the outline's evidence. Present analyzing, making, and receiving as available modes. A child's wonder and an experienced researcher's deliberate control need not occupy opposite rungs of maturity.

29. **Treat cross-domain MMM matches as comparisons.** Chapters 18 and 21 call MMM the same process across life; the wiki cites an AI paper as independent corroboration. [Dupoux, LeCun, and Malik](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15381) propose observational learning, active learning, and meta-control, which are a different partition. Compare functions and differences explicitly. Separate evolutionary population change from an individual's learning. A shared three-part description is insufficient evidence of the same mechanism.

30. **Start — Remove the unsupported mental-health and therapy comparisons.** Chapter 23 says weekly reflection changes more than most therapies; chapter 18 attributes most nonacute suffering to stalled loops; chapter 3 minimizes exhaustion in burnout. These claims outrun the evidence supplied, despite the existing clinical-care caveat. Offer reflection as a tool for some kinds of stuckness and include external causes and support. [WHO's account of burnout](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases) explicitly includes exhaustion and workplace stress.

31. **Give money and circumstances their full causal weight.** Chapter 23 says better living with money is almost always better modelling rather than more money, and career suffering rarely needs a new job. Those generalizations disregard unmet needs and harmful environments. Use two cases: someone choosing among affordable priorities and someone unable to cover essentials. [Income and well-being research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10013834/) also resists the simple money-does-not-matter story; its associations do not establish that any particular income change causes happiness.

32. **Add ethical premises beyond keeping loops running.** Chapters 20, 24, and the epilogue need an explicit bridge from effective adaptation to living well. An exploitative institution can learn efficiently. Keep your existing attention to participation and distribution, then ask about consent, preventable harm, fairness, and whose future choices remain open. State these as ethical commitments to discuss; they do not follow from feedback alone.

33. **Update the strength of the Becker/TMT evidence.** The denial-of-death wiki calls Terror Management Theory especially well supported and treats it as confirmation. A [2025 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40193452/) finds heterogeneous evidence, underpowered studies, and possible spurious effects while allowing some nonzero effects. Present Becker as an influential interpretation and TMT as contested empirical work. Avoid declaring either that mortality explains all meaning or that failed replications refute every part of Becker.

34. **Allow loss, deterioration, and unfinished outcomes.** Chapter 18 says every loop returns higher; chapter 19 says knowledge cannot be unmade by forgetting. Loops can entrench mistakes, and skills, records, and institutions can become unavailable or disappear. Distinguish an irreversible historical effect from continued access to knowledge. Ask what must be maintained. Chapter 21 already rejects inevitable growth in complexity; use that care consistently.

### Practicality — 35–40

35. **Start — Carry one ordinary situation through the whole book.** Use organizing a dinner: competing wishes, assumptions about guests, labels, a budget, recipes, coordination, surprise, and cleanup. Each act should reveal something new about it. Add occasional contrasting cases so a friendly dinner does not become the assumed structure of every human problem. This supplies continuity across the current conceptual outline.

36. **Move a complete worked application near the beginning.** Chapter 23 promises everyday examples but mostly supplies compressed claims about them. Finish one case with the original problem, the reader's initial interpretation, a competing account, a choice, an outcome, and a revision. Link it from the opening and revisit it later. A worked case can demonstrate the promise before readers traverse 22 chapters.

37. **Teach readers to identify what kind of response is needed.** In chapters 17 and 23, include “understand more,” “try something,” “change conditions or seek help,” “repair a relationship,” and “live with a loss.” A simple mode chooser should leave room for several responses and for uncertainty. Otherwise every difficult life situation becomes a reason to manipulate the reader's own thinking.

38. **Make room for care, maintenance, rest, and enough.** Chapters 3, 16, 18, and 23 repeatedly describe progress through growth or greater capacity. Show a good evening that produces nothing and a necessary maintenance task that preserves something valued. Help readers distinguish intrinsic enjoyment, obligation, learning, and ambition. Receptivity need not earn its place by producing a later breakthrough.

39. **Give every detour an explicit reason to matter.** Chapters 8, 12, 18, and 21 carry substantial taxonomies, mathematics, AI, and biological comparisons. Retain a main-path passage when it changes a reader's interpretation or judgment; otherwise offer an optional deep dive. Compress repeated lists of cycles and the cosmic ladder. Preserve wonder as a legitimate purpose, and identify when that is the purpose.

40. **End with an ordinary-language action and its limits.** Give readers a usable sentence: “I noticed __; I assumed __; what am I missing?” Or: “Here is one change I can try; here is when I will reassess.” Specify when the practice fits, how long to spend, and an acceptable stopping point. A reader should be able to use the lesson tomorrow without remembering an acronym.

### Interactive Ideas — 41–50

Each idea below produces something the reader can inspect or revisit. These are proposed exercises, not validated measures of wisdom or clinical improvement. Allow fictional examples, skipping, and private writing. Saving personal reflections should be under the reader's control. Any score should concern an observable task, not ideological agreement or spiritual development.

41. **Start — Build a returning “what happened?” notebook.** **Prologue, chapters 4, 6, 24.** Ask for a small recent event, then separate observation, interpretation, unknowns, and confidence in one factual claim. Reopen the same entry at the end. Output: a before-and-after account. Progress might be a better explanation, a narrower claim, or a useful unresolved question.

42. **Let competing wants share the screen.** **Chapters 1–3, 7.** Offer movable cards for immediate urge, expected enjoyment, obligation, fear, and chosen value. Readers can add their own or leave a card blank. Use a proposed purchase or invitation. Output: one conflict the reader can name and one way to honor more than one concern. Avoid pretending the tool discovers a hidden authentic desire.

43. **Replay a disagreement with missing information.** **Chapters 2, 4, 24.** Show a short fictional exchange and ask for two possible accounts. Reveal an extra constraint, such as a missed bus, and ask what changes. For a personal example, label the other person's motives as guesses. Output: one sincere question that could resolve uncertainty. The exercise should allow justified disagreement after clarification.

44. **Make a map's omissions matter.** **Chapters 5–6, 11.** Give readers a route with cost, time, steps, and accessibility. Let them change what the map emphasizes, predict a consequence, and uncover an omitted detail. Output: “This map is useful for __ and hides __.” Inspired by [explorable explanations](https://blog.ncase.me/how-i-make-an-explorable-explanation/), the model should make its assumptions visible.

45. **Build a label laboratory.** **Chapters 8–11.** Present the same behavior under “lazy,” “overloaded,” and “uninterested,” then ask what evidence each interpretation needs. Let readers replace a trait label with a specific observation. A second round adds a form with inadequate categories. Output: a more precise description and a question about what the category excludes.

46. **Let readers make rules for a shared garden.** **Chapters 9, 19–20.** In a small simulation, change access, maintenance duties, how damage is reported, and who can revise rules. Show multiple outcomes and delayed costs. Output: a rule proposal with a named tradeoff. Draw on [Ostrom](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.100.3.641), while stating that the simulation's encoded rules generate its results and cannot prove a political conclusion.

47. **Run a prediction–test–revision exercise.** **Chapters 12, 14, 24.** Let readers predict which change will affect a fictional bread recipe, select a comparison, and inspect results that include ordinary variation. Then design one low-stakes test of their own. Output: prediction, observation, alternative explanation, and revised confidence. Show why a single successful prediction is weak support when several explanations fit.

48. **Practise choosing a mode, then questioning the goal.** **Chapters 13–18.** Present situations such as planning a meal, overplanning an apology, or struggling to enjoy a walk. Ask whether more information, a small act, receptivity, rest, or help would serve. Reveal plausible tradeoffs rather than a universal correct mode. Output: a chosen next move and what would make the reader choose differently.

49. **Offer two ways of attending to one ordinary object.** **Chapters 16, 22.** First describe a cup by its use; then look at its color, texture, wear, and associations without trying to improve anything. Readers can decline the exercise or report no difference. Output: an account of what attention changed and what the exercise cannot establish. This can illustrate a practice without claiming to prove a metaphysics.

50. **Start — End with one experiment and a later return.** **Chapter 23 and epilogue.** Readers choose one small change, give an if–then cue, write an expectation and a limit, and set a revisit date. On return, ask what happened, what else may explain it, and where the book's frame failed. Provide an exportable note. Track the reader's ability to apply, question, and revise an idea; completing the book or agreeing with MMM is insufficient evidence of inner progress.

## Suggested first revision

First finish the opening sample and homepage promise (**1–3**), reconcile the framework versions (**5**), and correct the claims that most affect trust (**21–22, 28–31**). Then carry one worked ordinary case through the acts (**35–36**) and build the returning notebook and week-long experiment (**41, 50**). Use a few readers' reactions (**10**) to decide which of the more expensive simulations to build.

The intended result is a reader who can notice an assumption, understand another plausible account, choose an appropriate response, and recognize where your framework stops helping. The book can invite a philosophical journey while leaving its destination open to honest disagreement.
