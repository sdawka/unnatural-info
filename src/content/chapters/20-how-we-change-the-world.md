The garden committee discovers that its new watering rule works. The tank lasts all week. Then someone notices why: the plots nearest the gate are thriving while the far beds receive weak pressure, and the gardener who uses a wheelchair cannot reach the new valve.

The system has adapted. It is not therefore good.

Feedback matters. A change that cannot register its harms cannot correct them. Reversibility matters too: pencil marks on a planting plan are easier to revise than concrete poured across the only accessible path. Participation matters because people living with consequences often know what a distant designer misses. Yet an exploitative institution may learn efficiently how to preserve itself. Capability is not moral progress with better stationery.

The committee might install an accessible handle now, test pressure at every plot, compensate the person doing the measurements, and set a date to reconsider allocation. Its reasons come from care, consent, preventable harm, fairness, and future choice. Those commitments do not emerge automatically from a successful feedback loop. They tell us what the loop should notice and what it must not trade away for a larger harvest.

A garden is small enough that the valve and the affected people can meet in the same afternoon. Changes at civilizational scale are harder to see. They spread through many decisions, cross borders, acquire uses their makers did not imagine, and settle into ordinary life before anyone can render a final verdict. Two communication technologies make the difficulty visible.

## Printing: the same page, many different worlds

In Mainz in 1454 or 1455, craftspeople produced the Gutenberg Bible with movable metal type. The surviving books resist the tidy story that printing instantly replaced an older world. The [Library of Congress notes](https://guides.loc.gov/gutenberg) that copies were still finished by hand, with initials and headings supplied by different artisans. A new capacity arrived inside an existing culture of paper, ink, skill, money, religion, and trade.

What changed was not simply that one inventor had a thought. Workshops could make many closely similar copies; printers chose what might sell; financiers accepted risks; carriers moved sheets; authorities licensed or seized them; readers gathered around them. Over decades, printing became an infrastructure for preserving and circulating claims. The result was more reach for scholarship, administration, devotion, argument, fraud, satire, and abuse. The press did not inspect a pamphlet and prefer truth.

Even apparently clean measures tell only part of the story. Economist Jeremiah Dittmar found that European cities adopting printing in the fifteenth century later grew faster than comparable cities in his analysis ([*Quarterly Journal of Economics*](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjr035)). That is evidence of economic consequence, not a verdict that printing caused every later intellectual or political transformation. Readers, institutions, conflicts, and local conditions carried the change.

Could the transformation be repaired? A mistaken sheet could be corrected in another edition. Laws, schools, libraries, and professional standards could respond to new problems. But printed accusations could travel beyond recall, and investments in presses and distribution altered who could speak at scale. Repair remained possible, but it was rarely a rewind. It meant building another practice in the world printing had helped make.

## The Internet and the Web: layers of possibility

The Internet is not the Web. The Internet connects networks through shared protocols; email and many other services use it. The Web is one system built on that infrastructure, using addresses, links, browsers, servers, and HTTP.

That distinction matters because neither arrived as a single finished object. An [Internet Society history written by people involved](https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/) describes research groups, public agencies, universities, companies, independent networks, evolving protocols, and a carefully planned transition to TCP/IP in 1983. No lone engineer pressed a large red **INTERNET** button, disappointing though that may be to museums.

Tim Berners-Lee proposed the Web at CERN in 1989 to help people working across different systems share information. CERN [released its Web software into the public domain in 1993](https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/licensing-web/). Berners-Lee later explained that a person could start a server without registering it with a central authority; this minimal constraint helped the Web scale ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/People/Berners-Lee/1996/ppf.html)). Open standards and permissionless publishing expanded what distant people could learn, make, coordinate, sell, and preserve.

Again, more capability did not choose its own good. The same reach can support mutual aid or harassment, public knowledge or organized deception, small publishers or systems that concentrate attention and surveillance. These outcomes were not all contained in the 1989 proposal, waiting to unfold. Governments, firms, standards bodies, maintainers, advertisers, communities, and billions of users kept changing the environment and one another.

Nor were the gifts or costs evenly shared. The International Telecommunication Union estimated that in 2024, 93 percent of people in high-income countries used the Internet, compared with 27 percent in low-income countries ([ITU Facts and Figures](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2024/11/10/ff24-internet-use/)). Being offline can close routes to work, education, public services, and relationships. Being online can expose a person to extraction, manipulation, fraud, and control. “Connect everyone” and “protect everyone” are not rival slogans so much as unfinished obligations.

Repair is possible, but no committee controls the whole. A harmful interface can change quickly; a dominant business model, installed cable, legal regime, or lost archive may be harder to alter. Open protocols can preserve room for alternatives, yet technical openness alone does not supply affordability, safety, democratic authority, or fair working conditions. Correction happens in layers, and sometimes one layer protects the problem in another.

The garden, printing, and the Internet differ enormously. Together they sharpen the same ethical questions: Who defines success? Who gains capacity? Who carries the labor and risk? Can affected people contest the arrangement? What evidence of harm can travel back? Which choices remain open to people who arrive later?

Learning helps detect consequences. Creating gives an arrangement a form the world can answer. Becoming can keep unplanned voices and values present. None guarantees goodness. Changing a shared world also requires care, consent, fairness, limits on preventable harm, and the humility to leave someone else a real chance to repair what we made.

The next chapter asks how far the book's pattern can travel. These cases offer a warning before it does: resemblance across scales may guide attention, but it must not erase differences in mechanism, agency, or power.
