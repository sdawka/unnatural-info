The garden committee discovers that its new watering rule works. The tank lasts all week. Then someone notices why: the plots nearest the gate are thriving while the far beds receive weak pressure, and the gardener who uses a wheelchair cannot reach the new valve.

The system has adapted. It is not therefore good.

Feedback matters. A change that cannot register its harms cannot correct them. Reversibility matters too: pencil marks on a planting plan are easier to revise than concrete poured across the only accessible path. Participation matters because people living with consequences often know what a distant designer misses. Yet none of these settles the ethical question. An exploitative institution may learn efficiently how to preserve itself.

We need commitments that do not fall out of the loop:

- **Care:** attend to flourishing, dependence, maintenance, and loss.
- **Consent:** do not treat participation as permission when refusal carries punishment.
- **Preventable harm:** ask who may be injured and what protection is owed before experimenting.
- **Fairness:** examine how benefits, risks, voice, and necessary labor are distributed.
- **Future choice:** avoid closing options for people who had no meaningful say.

These commitments can conflict. An urgent repair may proceed before a perfect consultation. A reversible trial can still impose unfair labor. Equal treatment can ignore unequal access. Ethics remains argument and judgment, not a score produced by five boxes.

Return to the valve. The committee might first install an accessible handle because delay is causing present exclusion, then test pressure at every plot, compensate the person doing the measurements, and set a date for gardeners to reconsider allocation. That response uses evidence, action, and openness. Its moral reasons come from accessibility, shared standing, and concern for harm—not from MMM itself.

Scale and speed change what caution requires. Large, durable interventions can spread hidden costs before feedback arrives. That gives a reason to preserve correction where possible. But haste is not inherently harmful. During a broken water main, waiting can destroy the garden. The relevant questions are what is known, what is urgent, who is exposed, what can be repaired, and who decides.

This modest case sits between personal self-help and declarations about civilization. Most world-changing happens through schedules, defaults, budgets, code, care work, and rules that determine whose inconvenience counts. The person cleaning after the meeting is changing the shared world too, though institutions often fail to name that labor.

Try drafting one rule for the garden. State the problem, who helped define it, who benefits, who pays, what evidence will be collected, and when the rule can change. Then add a right that cannot be traded away merely because the garden produces more tomatoes.

Learning helps us detect consequences. Creating gives proposed arrangements a form the world can answer. Becoming can keep unplanned voices and values present. Ethics asks what these capacities should serve. The question grows stranger when we compare them with processes beyond human institutions.
