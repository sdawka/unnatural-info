The dairy-free main course begins as a grand idea: perhaps a complicated mushroom pie. Then you find half a bag of rice, two carrots, lentils, and forty minutes.

Creating is where an intention meets a cupboard.

**Map** means finding your bearings in relation to a purpose. You need to know who will eat, what is available, how long it takes, and what cannot be compromised. This is not a complete model of cooking. It is a selective account of the terrain relevant now. A useful map includes constraints that enthusiasm would prefer to omit.

**Move** means making contact before certainty arrives. You rinse the lentils, taste the stock, and text the guest to check whether coconut milk works. The first move can be deliberately cheap: sketch the seating plan in pencil; cook one spoonful of the sauce; ask one sincere question before composing the perfect apology. Motion reveals features that planning alone cannot.

**Make** means leaving a change in the world. Dinner exists. A draft can be read. A repaired hinge holds the door. The result also pushes back. The rice is too soft, the guest dislikes coconut, the apology explains when it should listen. Making is not the victorious final stage. It is a way of acquiring consequences sturdy enough to answer us.

The three moves overlap. Skilled makers observe and explain constantly. They also receive accidents. The names are useful because they expose characteristic absences: a plan with no attempt, frantic effort with no bearings, or endless motion that never becomes an inspectable result.

Vague intentions often fail at the threshold. *I should listen better* does not specify when or how. Research on implementation intentions suggests that an if-then plan can help in some settings: *If I notice myself preparing a rebuttal, then I will first summarize what I heard.* This is neither a spell nor a substitute for care. The cue may never be noticed, the action may be wrong for the moment, and another person is not a project to optimize. It simply places a small handle where intention often slips.

Choose one low-stakes change for the coming week. Give it a cue, one manageable action, a limit, and a date for review. For example: *If I put the kettle on after dinner, I will clear one section of the counter for five minutes; I stop when the timer sounds; on Sunday I will check whether the space stayed usable.* Include the stopping rule. Creating without enough can turn every life into an unfinished renovation.

Some worthwhile activity preserves rather than produces: washing the pot, maintaining a friendship, replacing soil after harvest. Some situations require resources or cooperation that personal effort cannot supply. The framework should reveal those conditions, not translate them into insufficient will.

And sometimes the best evening contains no improvement project at all. The third mode begins when usefulness loosens its grip.
