You have thought carefully about dinner. The soup is too salty anyway. One guest cannot eat dairy. Meanwhile, somebody has started telling a story and, for once, nobody seems to be looking at a phone.

The plan has met a kitchen. Several kinds of attention are now available.

The soup invites investigation. How salty is it? Did the stock contain more salt than expected? Would dilution help? You observe, form an explanation, and try a careful change. Call this **Learning**: Measure, Model, Manipulate—or, in ordinary language, observe, explain, test. The last word means intervention, not domination. A test can improve understanding even when control is impossible.

The dairy problem calls for making. You check what is in the cupboard, decide which dishes can change, and assemble an alternative. Call this **Creating**: Map, Move, Make—find your bearings, try a route, produce something that can meet the world. You may learn while doing it, but the immediate test is practical: can your guest eat?

The conversation asks something else of you. You could keep comparing the evening with the one in your plan. Or you could put the spoon down and listen. A story may wander somewhere nobody intended. You can make room for that without knowing what useful result it will produce. Call this **Becoming**: Marvel, Meander, Manifest—attend, explore, make room. Here *manifest* means supporting conditions in which something may emerge. You can set a table and give people time; you cannot order a worthwhile evening from the kitchen.

These three alliterative trios form the family we will call **MMM**. The name is a memory aid; reality has not signed an agreement to arrive in threes. The modes overlap. Tasting the soup is observation and skilled making. Inventing a dairy-free dish requires a model. An unexpected conversation may lead to a new plan. Nor do the modes rank people. A child can marvel; an experienced scientist can manipulate a variable; either can make badly or attend beautifully.

The distinction helps when one good habit has become automatic. Suppose you answer work messages faster and faster. That may solve the immediate problem of a crowded inbox. But perhaps the goal itself—permanent availability—deserves examination. Chris Argyris called the difference between correcting action and questioning its governing assumptions single-loop and double-loop learning. MMM adds a plain-language prompt: do I need to understand this, make something, receive what I cannot yet organize, or reconsider what I am trying to achieve?

There are answers outside the three modes. You may need rest, another person's help, a material resource, a firm boundary, or time to grieve. A framework becomes silly when every locked door is treated as a puzzle in the user's mind.

Try [One sheet, three kinds of doing](/practice/modes). With the same piece of paper, test an explanation, make a small object, and follow a shape without deciding its purpose. The kitchen contains all three sorts of activity; paper lets you slow them down. Notice what you count as a result in each pass. Then ask what the categories themselves leave out.

We begin with Learning, because the soup is still on the stove.
