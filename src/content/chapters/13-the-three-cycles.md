At six twenty, the soup is too salty, one guest has texted that she cannot eat dairy, and the person you most hoped would come has still not answered. Three problems have arrived in one kitchen, but they are not asking for the same kind of response.

The soup invites investigation. How salty is it? Did the stock contain more salt than expected? Would dilution help? You observe, form an explanation, and try a careful change. Call this **Learning**: Measure, Model, Manipulate—or, in ordinary language, observe, explain, test. The last word means intervention, not domination. A test can improve understanding even when control is impossible.

The dairy problem calls for making. You check what is in the cupboard, decide which dishes can change, and assemble an alternative. Call this **Creating**: Map, Move, Make—find your bearings, try a route, produce something that can meet the world. You may learn while doing it, but the immediate test is practical: can your guest eat?

The unanswered invitation is different. More analysis may only manufacture stories. More action may become pressure. For a while, the decent response may be to leave space: notice the disappointment, remain open to explanations you do not have, and let the evening be larger than one reply. Call this **Becoming**: Marvel, Meander, Manifest—attend, explore, make room. Here *manifest* means supporting conditions in which something may emerge. It does not mean wishing an outcome into existence.

These three alliterative trios form the family we will call **MMM**. The name is a memory aid, not evidence that reality itself comes in threes. The modes overlap. Tasting the soup is observation and skilled making. Inventing a dairy-free dish requires a model. Making room for an absent guest may eventually lead to a direct conversation. Nor do the modes rank people. A child can marvel; an experienced scientist can manipulate a variable; either can make badly or attend beautifully.

The distinction helps when one good habit has become automatic. Suppose you answer work messages faster and faster. That may solve the immediate problem of a crowded inbox. But perhaps the goal itself—permanent availability—deserves examination. Chris Argyris called the difference between correcting action and questioning its governing assumptions single-loop and double-loop learning. MMM adds a plain-language prompt: do I need to understand this, make something, receive what I cannot yet organize, or reconsider what I am trying to achieve?

There are answers outside the three modes. You may need rest, another person's help, a material resource, a firm boundary, or time to grieve. A framework becomes silly when every locked door is treated as a puzzle in the user's mind.

Try the distinction on one small difficulty. Name what would count as better evidence, one useful attempt, and one thing you may need to allow rather than fix. Then ask the harder question: why is this the goal? The answers need not agree. Their disagreement is often the point.

We begin with Learning, because the soup is still on the stove.
