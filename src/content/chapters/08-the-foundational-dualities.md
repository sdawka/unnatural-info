At a crowded table, someone says, “Are you with us or against us?” The sentence is efficient. It turns a messy room into two chairs. It may also make honest speech impossible.

Pairs are among our oldest tools. Near and far. Mine and yours. Signal and noise. Safe and dangerous. The three blades introduced self/world, map/territory, and approach/avoidance because each helps a finite creature do something: protect a body, revise an expectation, choose where to put its energy. A duality is a handle. It gives thought somewhere to grip.

The trouble begins when a handle is mistaken for the shape of the whole object. Most useful categories have middles, borderlands, exceptions, and overlapping uses. “Home” can mean a building, a person, a country, or a body that feels safe. “Public” and “private” are not natural bins when a family argument travels through a shared phone. A person can be loyal to a group and dissent from its current decision. The world is often more continuous and entangled than a clean pair suggests.

This does not mean we should stop distinguishing. A hospital must distinguish medication from poison. A friend may need to distinguish a joke from cruelty. Sometimes one side really is better supported by evidence or kinder in consequence. The discipline is to ask what the pair is for, what evidence it uses, and who gets injured by its leftovers.

Social dualities are particularly combustible because they organize belonging. “Logical” and “emotional” can make someone’s fear sound irrelevant before anyone has heard it. “Normal” and “abnormal” can provide access to care and also turn a variation into a stain. “Us” and “them” can support solidarity against real harm and can erase people who live across the line. The same category may protect in one setting and punish in another.

There is a fair objection: if every distinction is softened, do we lose the nerve to judge? No. Soft edges do not require mushy judgment. They require reasons proportionate to the consequence. If we are deciding who gets a scarce resource, which rule applies, or whether someone has caused harm, the question becomes more urgent, not less: what exactly are we grouping together, and what would show that the grouping fails?

Try [The cup changes jobs](/practice/labels). Keep the same chipped cup in view while calling it a utensil, a keepsake, and shop stock. Notice which action each description makes sensible. Then ask what happens when a person becomes a category on a form. The cut has begun doing work.

Once cuts become repeatable, shareable, and named, they acquire an odd stability. They begin to look like things.
