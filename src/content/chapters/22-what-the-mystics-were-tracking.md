A cup breaks at breakfast.

Epictetus might direct attention toward the judgment surrounding the loss. Objects are breakable; other people and events are not ours to command; our own judgments and chosen responses are where practice begins. This is not indifference. Stoic exercises aim to reshape how a person assents, acts, and understands their place among others.

A Buddhist practitioner working with the *Satipatthana Sutta* encounters a different field: body, feelings, mind, and patterns of experience are observed as they arise and pass. The practice belongs to a path concerned with suffering, craving, impermanence, ethics, and liberation. It is not merely calming down about crockery.

Both traditions train attention and connect repeated practice with a way of living. That comparison is worthwhile. Their accounts of self, agency, emotion, and freedom also differ. Saying that they are “really” running MMM would replace two traditions with our silhouette of them.

We can instead make a limited translation. Epictetus's examination of impressions may resemble Learning: notice a reaction, examine the judgment, test another response. Mindfulness may resemble Marvel in its disciplined receptivity. Daily exercises and ethical conduct have a Creating aspect because practices take form in action. These are this book's interpretations. They neither validate MMM nor exhaust what practitioners believe is happening.

The caution applies to the word *mystic*. It can gather contemplatives, philosophers, ritual specialists, healers, and communities into one romantic character. Traditions disagree internally as well as with one another. A monastery, Stoic notebook, Sufi gathering, and Indigenous ceremony cannot responsibly be reduced to “attention, model, manifestation” without studying the aims, authority, history, and obligations of each.

Practices can also have costs. Meditation is not universally benign; research includes reports of difficult or adverse experiences, and their frequency and meaning vary with definitions, settings, and populations. Community can offer teaching and support, and can also exercise pressure or exclusion. The relevant outcome must be named before declaring one method effective.

Try a modest exercise rather than a spiritual verdict. Look at a cup first through use: what does it hold, and what task does it serve? Then attend to texture, wear, temperature, memory, and your changing reactions. You may notice more. You may feel bored. Neither response demonstrates impermanence, Stoic agency, enlightenment, or an information realm.

Pierre Hadot described ancient philosophy as a way of life formed through exercises. That insight fits this book's practical concern: ideas alter lives when embodied in habits, relationships, and institutions. But the content of the practice matters. What a person attends to, why, under whose guidance, and toward which good cannot be supplied by the generic fact of repetition.

The fairest meeting with a tradition begins before analogy: What problem does this practice name? What does it ask of a person? What would its own practitioners count as misunderstanding? Only then should MMM enter the room.
