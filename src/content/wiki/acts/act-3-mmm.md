# Act 3 — How we learn, create, and change

> MMM is the book's family nickname for three overlapping modes: observe, explain, and test; find your bearings, try, and make; attend, explore, and make room.

## Overview

Act 3 asks what oriented beings do with the distinctions they have made. It does not propose a maturity ladder or one mechanism running identically from cells to civilizations. The three modes are a practical synthesis: they overlap, interrupt one another, and sometimes fail.

**Learning** asks what can be known. In plain language: observe differences, build an explanation, and test it. Testing can involve intervention, comparison, or a careful return to the evidence; learning does not always deliver control.

**Creating** asks what can be made. Find your bearings, try a move, and make something that can meet the world. A prototype or conversation can teach before the full plan exists.

**Becoming** asks what needs attention and room. Attend to what is present, explore possibilities without fixing the outcome too early, and support conditions for something to emerge. “Manifest” never means wishing an outcome into existence.

Chapters 12–24 move from knowledge to experimentation, social artifacts, world-changing, life, contemplative practice, daily problems, and objectivity. The comparisons to cybernetics, learning theory, pragmatism, and wisdom traditions are analogies and working tools. Similar three-part descriptions in other fields do not independently validate the whole framework.

The scale widens through the act. Chapter 19 moves from a garden to an imagined public water network, where records, pipes, skilled work, and authority must keep meeting. Chapter 20 examines printing and the Internet as collective changes whose reach does not settle their goodness. Chapter 21 follows biological dependence into symbolic culture: a language, tool, song, or warning can make something available beyond a single lifetime, through mechanisms distinct from biological evolution.

## Open questions

- What does this synthesis add to existing feedback, learning, and practice models?
- How do we distinguish improvement from entrenching a harmful goal?
- Which forms of change require collective action, material support, or acceptance rather than a new inner model?
