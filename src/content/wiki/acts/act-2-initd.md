# Act 2 — How we make sense of things

> Finite observers must select what to notice. Boundaries, maps, values, and categories make action possible; they also hide detail and distribute consequences.

## Overview

The act begins with an ordinary problem: a plan cannot include every fact about a situation. You choose a route, a budget, a seating arrangement, or a description because attention and time are limited. The practical point is simply that more detail is available than this observer can use right now.

The old title `init.d` remains as a private metaphor for booting a workable process. The public route is **How we make sense of things**.

Chapters 5–7 introduce three useful cuts: self/world, world/model, and good/bad. Chapter 8 asks whether a distinction is a binary, a gradient, or a set of overlapping categories. Chapter 9 follows names into forms, roles, money, and law. Chapter 10 separates physically carried representations from the reality they describe, then traces how organized practices can have physical effects. Chapter 11 asks who pays when a simplification becomes a target or an identity.

The act's sources include Spencer-Brown, Maturana and Varela, Gibson, Korzybski, Searle, Bowker and Star, Ostrom, and Whitehead. They offer different accounts of boundaries and institutions. None licenses the claim that a map is the only thing a person has, or that a useful category is the world itself.

## Open questions

- When does a representation guide skilled action without functioning like an explicit internal map?
- Which categories are maintained by institutions, and which are robust without agreement?
- How can a useful distinction remain revisable instead of hardening into an essence?
