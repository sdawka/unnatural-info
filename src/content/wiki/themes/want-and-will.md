# Want and will

The book uses **want** for a particular pull, preference, care, or aversion in a situation. It uses **will** more broadly for a system's orientation toward conditions it approaches, avoids, preserves, or changes. Neither term means conscious choice by definition.

Chapter 1 places the functional lens before reflective thought while keeping experience questions open. A bacterium's movement toward a chemical gradient is not evidence that it feels hunger. A person's urge, enjoyment, obligation, fear, and endorsed value can point in different directions. The [wants practice](/practice/wants) makes those conflicts visible without promising to reveal one authentic self underneath them.

Chapter 2 treats difference as a practical consequence of situated orientations, not as proof that every perspective is equally true. Chapter 3 connects care and meaning while preserving rest, loss, constraint, and moral disagreement. Becker's mortality account is one possible pressure on human projects, not the universal explanation of wanting.

## Open questions

- When does orientation become feeling or consciousness?
- Which wants deserve endorsement, and what reasons support that judgment?
- How much of a person's direction comes from biology, history, institutions, and material conditions?
