# Cost of distinction

Every category gains leverage by leaving something out. A route can be followed because it omits most of the city. A workplace metric can be compared because it compresses care, quality, and context into a number. A label can coordinate support and still become a false essence.

The cost is not an abstract infinity claim. It is the practical gap between what a distinction was made for and what it is later asked to carry. A map made for speed may fail accessibility. A form made for payroll may misdescribe a person's role. A score rewarded as a target may drift away from the value it was meant to track.

Ask four questions: What does this cut help us do? What does it hide? Who bears the mismatch? Who can revise the category? Bowker and Star's work on classification, James C. Scott's work on legibility, and Ostrom's work on shared rules offer concrete ways to ask them.

Try the [labels practice](/practice/labels) or [garden practice](/practice/garden). The aim is not to abandon categories. It is to keep them answerable to the people and situations they organize.
